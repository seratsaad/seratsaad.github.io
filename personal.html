The longer version.

Mirpur, Dhaka

I grew up in Mirpur — a dense, loud, wonderfully chaotic neighborhood in the heart of Dhaka, Bangladesh. If you've never been, imagine a place where ten million people live within a few square kilometers, where the streets never sleep, where the call to prayer mixes with rickshaw bells and street vendors selling jhalmuri at midnight. It's about as far from a quiet American suburb as you can get, and I wouldn't trade it for anything.

Home. Mirpur, Dhaka.

Cadet college

My parents had a plan for me: the Bangladesh Armed Forces. Clear path, respected career, stable future. When I was in grade 7, they sent me to Mirzapur Cadet College — a military boarding school where discipline was the curriculum and the day started before the sun did.

Every morning at 5:30 AM, rain or shine, we ran 4 kilometers before breakfast. I learned to iron a uniform so crisp it could stand on its own, to march in formation until my legs went numb, and to function on four hours of sleep. The military teaches you that discomfort is temporary and complaining is pointless — lessons that, it turns out, are excellent preparation for a Ph.D.

Mirzapur Cadet College.

But somewhere between the morning runs and the parade drills, I started looking up. Literally. The sky over Mirzapur was darker than anything I'd seen in Dhaka, and for the first time in my life I could actually see the Milky Way. I started reading about stars, then about galaxies, then about the universe itself. And slowly, quietly, a different plan started forming.

The Olympiad years

Telling my parents I didn't want to join the military was one of the hardest conversations of my life. They had invested years — emotionally, financially — in this path. But I had fallen in love with physics and astronomy, and I couldn't pretend otherwise.

I threw myself into the Olympiad world. The Bangladesh Olympiad on Astronomy and Astrophysics became my second home — a community of students who stayed up until 3 AM arguing about orbital mechanics and who understood why someone would choose stars over a stable career. The Olympiad gave me purpose, mentors, lifelong friends, and eventually the confidence to aim for something bigger.

Olympiad days.

Nashville

I applied to universities in the US not knowing what to expect. When the acceptance from Vanderbilt came, it felt unreal — a kid from Mirpur heading to Nashville, Tennessee. The culture shock was spectacular. I went from a country of 170 million people to a campus where strangers said "how are you?" and actually waited for an answer.

Vanderbilt is where I became a scientist. I double-majored in Physics and Mathematics, minored in Astronomy and Scientific Computing, and discovered that research is just problem-solving with higher stakes and longer timescales. I found young stars in spectroscopic data, chased black hole binaries through UV spectra, co-founded a quantum computing education program, and somehow survived organic chemistry (the less said about that, the better).

Vanderbilt University, Nashville.

Vanderbilt is also where I met my wife, Subha Mostafiz — which, in the long run, may turn out to be the most important discovery I've made.

With Subha.

Family

None of this would have happened without my parents and sisters. My parents bet everything on my education — even when I took that bet in a direction they didn't expect. My sisters have been my earliest and most honest critics (they still are). I carry all of them with me, even when home is 8,000 miles away.

Home team.

Now

I'm in Columbus, Ohio, doing a Ph.D. in Astronomy at Ohio State. I spend my days measuring the velocities of stars that are impossibly far apart, trying to figure out if gravity works the way we think it does. It's a long way from morning runs at cadet college — but honestly, the discipline stuck. The 5:30 AM wake-ups are optional now, though. I choose to sleep in.
